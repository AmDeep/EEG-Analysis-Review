## Abstract

Pain perception varies considerably between and within individuals. How the brain determines these variations has yet to be fully understood. The peak frequency of alpha oscillations has recently been shown to predict an individual's sensitivity to longer-lasting experimental and clinical pain. Peak alpha frequency (PAF) is, thus, discussed as a potential biomarker and novel target for neuromodulatory treatments of pain. Here, we scrutinized the generalizability of the relation between PAF and pain. We applied brief painful laser stimuli to 159 healthy participants and related interindividual and intraindividual variations of pain perception to PAF measured with electroencephalography. Comprehensive multiverse analyses replicated across 2 sessions did not provide consistent evidence for a predictive role of PAF for brief experimental pain. This indicates that the relationship between PAF and pain does not generalize to all types of pain and calls for a systematic exploration of the relationship between PAF, pain perception, and other neuropsychiatric symptoms. Such explorations help to understand the prospects and limits of biomarkers and might guide future research on biomarkers of pain and neuropsychiatric disorders.

## 1\. Introduction

Pain perception varies both between and within individuals, ie, from person to person and from moment to moment.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R7), [40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R40), [60](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R60) The neural mechanisms underlying such interindividual and intraindividual variations of pain are not fully known. Understanding them promises basic insights into the brain mechanisms of pain and could help to define new targets for pain treatment, eg, through neuromodulation.[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R30), [51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R51), [67](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R67) Moreover, as variations in pain sensitivity can predict the development of chronic pain[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R20), [42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R42) and responses to pain treatments,[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R11), [22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R22), [68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R68) identifying the associated neural mechanisms might help to develop clinically useful biomarkers of pain.[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R8)

In the brain, the perception of pain is associated with neuronal oscillations at different frequencies.[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R28), [50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R50), [66](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R66) Recently, the peak frequency of alpha oscillations (peak alpha frequency \[PAF\]) in sensorimotor regions has been related to interindividual variations of pain.[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R35), [61](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R61) Peak alpha frequency (Fig. [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#F1)) is measured using spectral analysis of brain activity recorded with electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG). It is commonly defined as the frequency with the highest peak in the alpha band (8-13 Hz).[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R47) In previous studies, a slower resting-state PAF was associated with higher pain ratings during experimental pain lasting from minutes to weeks,[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R13)– [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R15) but see [Ref. 43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R43) for a positive relation. Most recently, these findings have been extended to clinical conditions. In people with lung cancer, preoperative resting-state PAF predicted pain severity after surgery.[37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R37)

## 3\. Results

One hundred fifty-nine healthy participants participated in 2 identical experimental sessions (Fig. [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#F3)), consisting of a resting-state recording followed by an experimental pain paradigm applying brief laser stimuli at 4 fixed intensities. Using EEG, PAF was estimated, and interindividual and intraindividual variations of PAF were related to interindividual and intraindividual variations of pain perception. In a 2-step approach, we first performed primary analyses, quantifying PAF using an analytical pipeline chosen a priori. Subsequently, we complemented primary analyses with specification curve analyses.[62](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#R62) For replication, we performed all analyses for sessions 1 and 2.

### 3.1. Interindividual and intraindividual variations of pain perception

We first confirmed that pain ratings in response to laser stimuli varied both between and within individuals using all trials from the experimental pain paradigm (Fig. [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363489/#F4)). For each participant, ratings were first averaged across trials for each of the 4 stimulus intensities separately and then averaged across intensities. Across participants, the average pain rating was 22 ± 15 (mean ± SD, ranging from 0 to 62) for session 1 and 22 ± 14 (1-69) for session 2.
