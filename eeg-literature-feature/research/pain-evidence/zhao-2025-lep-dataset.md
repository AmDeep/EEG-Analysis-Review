## Abstract

Understanding the neural mechanisms of pain is key to the development of novel pain diagnostic and treatment strategies. Here, we present a large-scale, comprehensive electroencephalogram (EEG) dataset of laser-evoked potentials (LEPs) from 678 healthy participants. This dataset comprises high-density EEG recordings and single-trial self-reported pain ratings in response to nociceptive laser stimuli of varying intensities (from 2.5 J to 4.5 J) delivered to either the left or right dorsum of the hand, without any physical or psychological interventions. As the largest nociceptive-evoked EEG dataset to date, it can serve as a valuable resource for mechanistic pain studies such as investigating electrophysiological underpinnings of within-individual and between-individual pain variations, for clinical studies such as providing normal values of LEPs to assess the possible lesions of pain pathways, and for methodological innovations in EEG signal processing such as optimizing preprocessing pipelines and developing new analytical tools.

**Subject terms:** Neuroscience, Psychology

## Background & Summary

Pain is a fundamental sensory and emotional experience crucial for survival, but this protective mechanism can become pathological in chronic pain conditions, leading to significant impairment in quality of life[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR1). Understanding the neural mechanisms of pain is key to the development of novel pain diagnostic and treatment strategies. Noninvasive brain imaging technologies such as electroencephalography (EEG) have led to remarkable advancements in elucidating the cortical processing of pain signals and the interplay between physiological and psychological processes in pain perception[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR2), [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR3). For example, nociceptive-evoked brain potentials like N1, N2, and P2 waves, and neural oscillations like alpha- and gamma-band oscillations have been shown to encode stimulus intensity[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR4), spatial information[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR5), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR6), and subjective pain perception[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR7). These responses have also been utilized to build models that accurately predict pain perception at both within and between individuals[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR8)– [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR10). In addition to pain perception, nociceptive-evoked EEG responses have also been associated with motor and autonomic responses to pain[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR11), [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR12) and could be influenced by various psychological factors like expectation on pain[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR13)– [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR15). Crucially, laser-evoked potentials (LEPs) can be used to assess the peripheral and central nervous system lesions of pain pathways[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR16), thus providing a reliable diagnostic tool in clinical applications[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR16).

Despite these advancements, the field of pain neuroimaging also faces significant challenges. One key challenge is that pain research is often hindered by small sample sizes. Many findings in this field have been based on sample sizes of fewer than 30 participants. Such small sample sizes can lead to serious problems, including insufficient statistical power, overestimation of effect sizes, and false positive or negative findings[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR17)– [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR19). A large-scale EEG dataset can help address these challenges by providing extensive, high-quality data that enhance the precision and scope of scientific research[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR

### Single trial behavioral data

During the preprocessing process, the stimulus intensity and subjective rating of pain perception for each single trial were recorded in each participant’s **.set** file. When the data is opened in EEGLAB, these values can be found in the **EEG.epoch** structure.

### EEG data

All data are named according to the experiments and identified as follows: 26\_ByBiosemi, 29\_ByANT, 39\_ByBP, 30\_ByANT, 65\_ByANT, 95\_ByBP, 142\_ByBiosemi, 223\_ByBP, and 29\_ByBP. The raw data are organized by subject participation and named as sub-xxx (where xxx is the subject number, starting from 001). For each subject, data are arranged by session and include original EEG data files (formats: **\_eeg.eeg/\_eeg.bdf**), along with the associated **\_channels.tsv,** **\_electrodes.tsv,** **\_events.tsv**, and **\_eeg.json** files. The **\_channels.tsv** file contains the names, types, and units of electrode channels; the **\_electrodes.tsv** file contains the electrode positions; the **\_events.tsv** file records the start and duration of each event, the sampling rate, and the type of markers; the **\_eeg.json** file provides details on the data collection institution, address, department, equipment used, EEG reference electrode, project name, number of electrodes, sampling type, sampling time, and sampling frequency.

## Technical Validation

Our technical validation includes quality control of behavioral data and EEG responses to nociceptive laser stimuli. The relationship between stimulus intensities (in unit of Joule \[J\]) and pain ratings is illustrated in Fig. [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Fig3), showing an increasing trend of higher pain ratings corresponding to higher stimulus intensities. These behavioral patterns offer valuable insights into how varying stimulus intensities influence subjective pain perception, supporting the validity of graded laser stimulus intensities in eliciting graded pain perception.
