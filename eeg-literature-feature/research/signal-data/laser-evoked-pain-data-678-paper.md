## Abstract

Understanding the neural mechanisms of pain is key to the development of novel pain diagnostic and treatment strategies. Here, we present a large-scale, comprehensive electroencephalogram (EEG) dataset of laser-evoked potentials (LEPs) from 678 healthy participants. This dataset comprises high-density EEG recordings and single-trial self-reported pain ratings in response to nociceptive laser stimuli of varying intensities (from 2.5 J to 4.5 J) delivered to either the left or right dorsum of the hand, without any physical or psychological interventions. As the largest nociceptive-evoked EEG dataset to date, it can serve as a valuable resource for mechanistic pain studies such as investigating electrophysiological underpinnings of within-individual and between-individual pain variations, for clinical studies such as providing normal values of LEPs to assess the possible lesions of pain pathways, and for methodological innovations in EEG signal processing such as optimizing preprocessing pipelines and developing new analytical tools.

**Subject terms:** Neuroscience, Psychology

## Background & Summary

Pain is a fundamental sensory and emotional experience crucial for survival, but this protective mechanism can become pathological in chronic pain conditions, leading to significant impairment in quality of life[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR1). Understanding the neural mechanisms of pain is key to the development of novel pain diagnostic and treatment strategies. Noninvasive brain imaging technologies such as electroencephalography (EEG) have led to remarkable advancements in elucidating the cortical processing of pain signals and the interplay between physiological and psychological processes in pain perception[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR2), [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR3). For example, nociceptive-evoked brain potentials like N1, N2, and P2 waves, and neural oscillations like alpha- and gamma-band oscillations have been shown to encode stimulus intensity[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR4), spatial information[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR5), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR6), and subjective pain perception[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR7). These responses have also been utilized to build models that accurately predict pain perception at both within and between individuals[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR8)– [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR10). In addition to pain perception, nociceptive-evoked EEG responses have also been associated with motor and autonomic responses to pain[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR11), [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR12) and could be influenced by various psychological factors like expectation on pain[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR13)– [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR15). Crucially, laser-evoked potentials (LEPs) can be used to assess the peripheral and central nervous system lesions of pain pathways[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR16), thus providing a reliable diagnostic tool in clinical applications[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR16).

Despite these advancements, the field of pain neuroimaging also faces significant challenges. One key challenge is that pain research is often hindered by small sample sizes. Many findings in this field have been based on sample sizes of fewer than 30 participants. Such small sample sizes can lead to serious problems, including insufficient statistical power, overestimation of effect sizes, and false positive or negative findings[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR17)– [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR19). A large-scale EEG dataset can help address these challenges by providing extensive, high-quality data that enhance the precision and scope of scientific research[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR20), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR21). Furthermore, the promise of personalized pain treatment remains unfulfilled. Identifying neural biomarkers of individual differences in pain perception is a crucial issue, as it would enable precise diagnosis and tailored therapeutic interventions[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR9), ultimately improving patient care. However, current research on pain biomarkers is fraught with difficulties. One major challenge is the low predictive performance of existing biomarker candidates, which limits their widespread applications[22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR22). In addition to basic research efforts aimed at uncovering pain mechanisms, scientists may also develop advanced technologies and methodologies, such as machine learning algorithms, to better analyze the complex associations between pain and neural responses, and to construct more reliable pain biomarkers.

Notably, large-scale EEG datasets have already played a pivotal role in advancing neuroscience research, particularly in elucidating neural mechanisms and enhancing disease diagnosis. For instance, the MODMA dataset—which comprises EEG and audio recordings from 108 clinically depressed patients and matched healthy controls[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR23)—has been employed to demonstrate the diagnostic potential of EEG-based methods for depression using diverse analytical techniques[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR24), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR25). Likewise, Kiessner _et al_. leveraged the TUH-EEG Corpus, which contains clinical EEG data from over 10,000 epilepsy patients[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR26), to show that larger datasets substantially improve the accuracy of pathology decoding from EEG signals, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of machine-learning-based diagnostic models[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR27), [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR28).

Similarly, establishing a comprehensive EEG dataset for pain research helps overcome these challenges and harnesses the potential of big data. To support research in uncovering the neural dynamics underlying pain perception and facilitate the development of more reliable pain biomarkers, we present a comprehensive LEP dataset. Our dataset includes EEG recordings and self-reported pain ratings from 678 healthy participants who received nociceptive laser stimuli of varying intensities (laser power: from 2.5 J to 4.5 J) and at different sites (left- or right-hand dorsum)[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR4), [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR10), [29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR29)– [31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR31). It focuses solely on non-intervention conditions to help provide normal nociceptive-evoked neural responses. Nociceptive laser stimulators, which emit heat radiation pulses, are a preferred method for inducing experimental pain, as they selectively activate nociceptive-specific Aδ and C fiber nerve endings in the superficial layers of the skin, without causing tactile interference[32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR32). This large-scale dataset enables the study of fundamental pain processing and provides insights into neural responses to standardized nociceptive stimuli. Potential applications include mechanistic pain studies, such as investigating the electrophysiological underpinnings of within-individual and between-individual pain variations. The dataset also supports methodological innovations in EEG signal processing, including the development of new analytical tools, such as computational models and machine learning algorithms. Beyond basic research, it can serve as a diagnostic tool for providing normal values of LEPs to assess the possible lesions of pain pathways. By deepening our understanding of pain processing, this dataset has the potential to significantly advance pain research, ultimately aiding therapeutic development and improving clinical outcomes.

## Methods

### Participants

The database comprises a total of 678 participants, distributed across 9 distinct experiments with varying sample sizes: 26 participants in Experiment 1[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR33), 29 in Experiment 2[34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR34), 39 in Experiment 3[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR35), 30 in Experiment 4[36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR36), 65 in Experiment 5[37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR37), 95 in Experiment 6[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR38), 142 in Experiment 7[39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR39), 223 in Experiment 8[40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR40), and 29 in Experiment 9[41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR41). Demographic information, including age and gender, was collected for each participant (Table [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Tab1)). None of the participants had a history of psychiatric disorders, drug abuse, neurosurgical interventions, or implantable medical devices. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the local ethics review committee (Southwest University for Experiments 6 and 9; Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences for Experiments 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8 \[ [H17025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/H17025), [H22032](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/H22032), [H17026](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/H17026), [H21103](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/H21103)\]). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All research data has been consented to by participants, with explicit agreement for public sharing. Data sharing follows the appropriate ethical approval procedures to ensure the privacy of participants is fully protected. Participants received financial compensation for their involvement. Prior to each experiment, participants were instructed to maintain a regular sleep-wake cycle and abstain from alcohol or excessive caffeine consumption for at least 24 hours before the experiment. To facilitate rapid EEG setup, participants were asked to refrain from using heavy makeup, facial moisturizers, or hair/scalp products. An overview of the study objectives, main findings, and related publications for each experiment is provided in Table [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Tab1).

### Demographic information

The demographic information of the participants can be found in the **participants.tsv** file included in the dataset. The data were extracted from paper records and manually entered into the file. Each column in the file is thoroughly explained in the **participants.json** file, which provides detailed definitions of the data elements. Among the participants, the average age of males is 22.00 ± 3.10 years (mean ± standard deviation \[SD\]), and the average age of females is 21.48 ± 3.03 years. This meticulous documentation ensures clarity and facilitates further analysis and research.

### Data collection design

For all experiments in the dataset, the trial structure was largely consistent (Fig. [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Fig1)). In each trial, participants were instructed to focus on the laser stimuli, keep their eyes open, and looked at the fixation point displayed on the screen. After each laser stimulation, participants had a few seconds to report their subjective pain intensity using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), either verbally or via a computer keypress. The NRS is an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable)[42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR42), [43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR43), with 4 indicating the pinprick pain threshold for most datasets[44](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR44), [45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR45). The next stimulation was then delivered after a random interval. Detailed information for each experiment is presented in Table [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Tab2).

### Laser stimulation

Nociceptive laser stimuli in all experiments were radiant heat pulses generated by an infrared neodymium yttrium aluminum perovskite (Nd:YAP) laser (Electronical Engineering, Italy). The laser emitted at a wavelength of 1.34 µm with a pulse duration of 4 ms[46](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR46). The laser pulse was applied to a pre-defined area on the dorsum of either the left or right hand. The He-Ne laser pulse was transmitted through an optical fiber and focused by lenses to a spot with a diameter of ~7 mm. The laser beam target was manually displaced by at least 1 cm in a random direction to prevent fatigue or sensitization of nociceptors.

### EEG data acquisition

The dataset utilized three different types of EEG acquisition devices across various experiments:

**Biosemi:** For Experiments 1 and 7, EEG was continuously recorded using the ActiveTwo AD-box (BioSemi, Netherlands) at a sampling rate of 1024 Hz from direct current. Participants wore an EEG cap fitted with 62-channel Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according to the international 10–20 system. Electrode impedance was kept below 10 kΩ. The Biosemi system employs a “zero reference” setup, replacing the traditional “ground” electrode with two separate electrodes: a Common Mode Sense active electrode and a Driven Right Leg passive electrode. These electrodes form a feedback loop, driving the participant’s average potential as close as possible to the Analog-to-Digital Converter reference, which is considered the amplifier’s “zero” (for more information, see [https://www.biosemi.com/faq/cms&drl.htm](https://www.biosemi.com/faq/cms&drl.htm)).

**ANT Neuro:** In Experiments 2, 4, and 5, we used the ANT Neuro EEG acquisition system (Eenschede, the Netherlands). Participants wore an EEG cap fitted with 32-channel Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according to the international 10–20 system, with a sampling rate set at 1000 Hz. The impedance between the scalp and electrodes was kept below 10 kΩ during acquisition, and CPz was used as the online reference.

**Brain Products:** In Experiments 3, 6, 8, and 9, EEG data were collected using the Brain Products EEG system (Germany). Participants wore an EEG cap fitted with 64-channel Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according to the international 10–20 system, with a sampling rate set at 1000 Hz. Impedance for all electrodes was maintained below 10 kΩ, with the tip of the nose serving as the reference electrode. Electro-oculographic (EOG) signals were also recorded to monitor eye movements and blinks.

### EEG data preprocessing

The preprocessing results are stored in the **derivatives** folder. The Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) data were imported into EEGLAB for merging and electrode localization (the localization files used can be found in the **code-preprocessing\_.m** file). The merged and localized data files ( **\*.set**) are saved in the **derivatives-session\_merged\_data** folder. The merged data includes all the raw data collected during the experiments, with some experiments with additional sensory modalities or interventions. However, only the data with nociceptive laser stimuli, either pre-intervention or non-intervention, were extracted for preprocessing and subsequent technical validation. The extracted data underwent high-pass filtering with a cutoff frequency of 1 Hz, low-pass filtering with a cutoff frequency of 100 Hz, and notch filtering from 48 Hz to 52 Hz. EEG epochs were extracted using a time window of 3000 ms (1000 ms before and 2000 ms after stimulus onset), and the baseline was corrected using the pre-stimulus interval (-1000 ms ~ 0 ms). Based on visual inspection, trials contaminated by artifacts due to gross movements were removed (manual removal numbers are recorded in the **CSV-preprocessing.csv** file). Additionally, trials contaminated by eye blinks and movements were corrected using the independent component analysis (ICA) algorithm “runica” in EEGLAB[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#CR47). We kept the data file that marked the ICA component to be removed in the **derivatives-mark\_ica** folder. To clearly display time-domain LEP responses, low-pass filtering at 30 Hz was applied. Re-referencing was performed using the average reference across the whole brain, with the re-referenced data files stored in the **derivatives-rerefer** folder.

## Technical Validation

Our technical validation includes quality control of behavioral data and EEG responses to nociceptive laser stimuli. The relationship between stimulus intensities (in unit of Joule \[J\]) and pain ratings is illustrated in Fig. [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12405539/#Fig3), showing an increasing trend of higher pain ratings corresponding to higher stimulus intensities. These behavioral patterns offer valuable insights into how varying stimulus intensities influence subjective pain perception, supporting the validity of graded laser stimulus intensities in eliciting graded pain perception.
