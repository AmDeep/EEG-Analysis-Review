## Abstract

Conditioned pain modulation (CPM) can discriminate between healthy and chronic pain patients. However, its relationship with neurophysiological pain mechanisms is poorly understood. Brain oscillations measured by electroencephalography (EEG) might help gain insight into this complex relationship.

### Objective

To investigate the relationship between CPM response and self-reported pain intensity in non-specific chronic low back pain (NSCLBP) and explore respective EEG signatures associated to these mechanisms.

### Design

Cross-sectional analysis.

### Participants

Thirty NSCLBP patients participated.

### Methods

Self-reported low back pain, questionnaires, mood scales, CPM (static and dynamic quantitative sensory tests), and resting surface EEG data were collected and analyzed. Linear regression models were used for statistical analysis.

### Results

CPM was not significantly correlated with self-reported pain intensity scores. Relative power of EEG in the beta and high beta bands as recorded from the frontal, central, and parietal cortical areas were significantly associated with CPM. EEG relative power at delta and theta bands as recorded from the central area were significantly correlated with self-reported pain intensity scores while controlling for self-reported depression.

### Conclusions

Faster EEG frequencies recorded from pain perception areas may provide a signature of a potential cortical compensation caused by chronic pain states. Slower EEG frequencies may have a critical role in abnormal pain processing.

**Keywords:** Chronic Low Back Pain, EEG Signature, Conditioned Pain Modulation, Pain Perception, Chronic Pain

## Introduction

Conditioned pain modulation (CPM) is used to assess endogenous central pain control mechanisms \[ [1–5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B1)\] and has been suggested as diagnostic marker of chronic pain and an indicator of clinical pain intensity and other pain-related symptoms in chronic pain trials \[ [6–8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B6)\]. Despite its frequent association with self-reported pain measures in healthy and pain conditions, a recent systematic review \[ [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B9)\] investigated the validity of CPM as a biomarker of chronic pain, and its correlations with pain-related measures. The authors found that 69% of the studies included in the analysis reported non statistically significant associations between CPM and clinical pain measures (e.g., intensity, duration). Although part of this result may be explained by the large variability of CPM testing protocols, chronic pain indications studied, and pain measures used among studies, taken together, the studies highlight that the relationship between CPM and chronic pain is not fully understood. Investigating whether and to what extent brain oscillations associated to CPM or self-report pain measures differ might help gain insight into the complex relationship between CPM and chronic pain and improve the ability of CPM to predict pain intensity.

Assessment of brain oscillations on chronic pain patients via electroencephalography (EEG) can help unveil patterns of pain-related brain activity. Studies have shown that chronic pain is associated with EEG changes in specific frequency ranges \[ [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B10)\]. For example, studies on rest-EEG power in migraine related, fibromyalgia pain, neuropathic pain, and spinal cord injury \[ [10–16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B10)\] have shown an increased theta power compared to healthy controls. Such finding may be explained by the thalamocortical dysrhythmia theory where an increased theta power is related to a poor inhibition of the thalamus \[ [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B17), [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B18)\]. While these studies provide evidence that chronic pain is associated to a clear increase in the EEG power at lower frequencies \[ [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B10)\], whether a similar change in EEG power also underlies CPM responses remains unclear.

Only a few studies have investigated cortical activation changes during CPM using EEG. Two studies \[ [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B19), [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B20)\] on healthy subjects investigated changes in temporal and spatial cortical activation using pain evoked potentials and LORETA source localization. During CPM, an increased activation in the orbito frontal cortex and amygdala was observed; these were proportional to reductions in subjective pain ratings and were followed by a decreased activation in several pain-perceiving and -processing cortex areas such as the supplementary motor area, primary and secondary somatosensory cortices, posterior insula, and anterior cingulate cortex. These reduced activations were not correlated with reductions in pain scores. However, these studies did not investigate spectral EEG power during resting-state. One other study in healthy subjects investigated pain-modulating mechanisms via EEG wavelet time frequency analysis and showed that the EEG signature during CPM was characterized by dominance of prefrontal electrodes, with dominant activity in the delta band \[ [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B21)\]. Finally, one study found a suppression on pain related evoked potentials in delta band during a CPM experiment in healthy subjects \[ [22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B22)\]. All the above studies only examined EEG changes during CPM tasks; therefore, it is unknown the association between resting state brain oscillations and the CPM response. Characterizing brain activity at rest is relevant as it reflects intrinsic activity of the brain and may provide information on how different brain areas communicate \[ [23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B23), [24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B24)\].

The relationship between CPM and resting state brain oscillations in chronic pain patients is poorly explored and consequently unclear. If distinct resting-state EEG patterns can be found for the CPM response and self-reported pain intensity, a better understanding of their relationship would be possible in this population. This information also could be used as the basis to build diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers for chronic pain studies. Besides, an EEG spectral power signature associated with CPM disruption could be used as bio signal to implement neurofeedback protocols targeting endogenous central pain control mechanisms and to optimize the available protocols \[ [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B25)\].

Our primary aim is to explore the relationship between CPM response and self-reported pain intensity in patients with non-specific chronic low back pain (NSCLBP); and secondarily, we also aim to explore whether there is a resting-state EEG signature associated with these variables. We hypothesized to find a significant negative correlation between CPM and pain intensity scores. In addition, we hypothesized that the resting-state EEG pattern associated with clinical pain scores is a higher theta band power due to a poor thalamus inhibition \[ [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B17), [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B18)\], and with CPM response is based on delta band oscillatory activity due to the need of cortical-subcortical inhibition\[ [26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B26), [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B27)\].

## Methods

### Study Design

This is an ancillary, exploratory study of a randomized clinical trial investigating non-invasive brain stimulation for the treatment of CLBP (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: [NCT02954432](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02954432)) where data from the baseline assessments were cross-sectionally analyzed.

### Setting

This study was conducted at Spaulding Rehabilitation Hospital in Charlestown Massachusetts, USA. Participants were recruited from the hospital network greater Boston metropolitan area through research patient registries, local newspaper and online advertisements and community flyers. All study procedures were approved by the Partner's Institutional Review Board at the Partners Human Research Committee (protocol approval number: 2016P001956) and written informed consent was obtained for all participants. In addition, this research complies with all the relevant national regulations, institutional policies and was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration.

### Participants

Thirty patients with NSCLBP participated in the study. To note, the sample reported on herein underwent baseline assessments for the [NCT02954432](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02954432) study (making up the data herein) but not all the participants qualified or joined the full [NCT02954432](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02954432) study. NSCLBP was diagnosed based on previously established criteria \[ [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B28), [29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B29)\]. Inclusion criteria for the clinical trial were: age between 18 and 85 years; NSCLBP for at least 3 months; existing pain of at least 3 points on a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS) for pain; pain resistant to common analgesics (such as acetaminophen, ibuprofen, aspirin, etc.) and medications for chronic pain; ability to feel the touch of a Von-Frey fiber on the forearm. Exclusion criteria were: clinically significant or unstable medical or psychiatric disorder; history of substance abuse within the past 6 months as self-reported; previous significant neurological history as self-reported (e.g., epilepsy, traumatic brain injury); previous neurosurgical procedure as self-reported; severe depression (established as a score of >30 on the Beck Depression Inventory) \[ [30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B30)\]; and pregnancy.

### Assessments

The assessments were collected during two baseline visits that occurred within a seven-day period (run-in phase) prior to the main study randomization. Self-reported questionnaires, VAS for pain and mood, and EEG recording were performed at the first baseline visit while quantitative sensory testing at the second. The main study ( [NCT02954432](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02954432)) eligibility criteria were assessed at both baseline visits and during the run-in phase and patients were excluded if criteria were not met. This study used a set of outcome measures for NSCLBP that have acceptable levels of validity, reliability, and sensitivity, following the recommendations in the ACTTION and IMMPACT guidelines for conducting clinical trials on chronic pain patients \[ [31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B31), [32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B32)\]. Demographic and disease related data were collected for all participants and the following test procedures were performed.

#### Self-Reported Questionnaires

Participants were asked to complete the National Institutes of Health (NIH) minimal data set questionnaire recommended by the NIH chronic low back pain research task force \[ [33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B33)\], and the anxiety, fatigue and pain interference sections of the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 29 instrument \[ [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B34)\]. Both instruments are designed to evaluate low back pain symptoms and its impact on physical and psychological function, sleep, and related activities.

##### NIH minimal data set

The NIH minimal dataset is a 40-item questionnaire composed from combined items of the PROMIS instrument, the Subgroups for Targeted Treatment (STarT) Back instrument \[ [35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B35)\], and the Research Task Force Impact classification\[ [36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B36)\]. The subscales of Pain Intensity and Interference, Depression and Catastrophizing, Physical Function, and Sleep Disturbances were used in our analysis \[ [37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B37), [38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B38)\]. The range of possible scores for each subscale are respectively 6–35, 4–22, 3–15, and 4–20, with lower scores representing the absence of the symptom under consideration. In addition, the general Impact Stratification Score from the NIH minimal dataset, was also considered for the analysis.

##### Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 29

The PROMIS 29 is a collection of short forms that contain a fixed number of items (4) for seven different domains related to quality of life and it is considered to be useful for research on chronic musculoskeletal pain population \[ [39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B39), [40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B40)\]. Possible scores for each domain range from 4 to 20 with higher scores representing worse symptomatology. Each domain of the PROMIS 29 has a norm-based score calculated so that the score of 50 represents the average of the reference population and 10 is the standard deviation. Thus, a score of 60 would mean that a person is 1 standard deviation above the reference population. For descriptive purposes, we will present the norm-based score and its standard error for each of the PROMIS 29 domains considered for this analysis.

#### Pain

Participants were asked to self-rate their low back pain level on a visual image of a 0–10 cm horizontal line, marked from 0 to 10 anchored by verbal descriptors at each symptom extreme (0 = no pain, 10 = very painful). Participants rated their pain perception by both saying out loud the scale value and pointing to the scale image. Raters instructed participants to rate their pain as an average value over the last seven days (VAS\_Avg) and at the time of the assessment. Due to the variability on intensity levels of momentary pain data in chronic low back pain, we focused on the VAS\_Avg herein \[ [41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B41)\]. The VAS for clinical pain holds adequate reliability (r = 0.79–0.92) and a valid measure of pain as shown in chronic musculoskeletal pain studies \[ [32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B32)\].

##### Visual Analog Mood Scale

As symptoms of depression, anxiety, stress and sleep disruption are commonly reported among chronic pain patients \[ [42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B42)\], the visual analog mood scale (VAMS) was used to assess mood disturbance. Participants were asked to self-rate their anxiety, depression, stress, and sleepiness. Each emotion was rated separately on a 10 cm line as described above for the VAS for pain. The anchors used at either end of the scale described the extreme of the emotion under study. Raters instructed participants to rate their emotions at the moment of the assessment. The VAMS has acceptable psychometric properties \[ [43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B43), [44](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B44)\].

#### Static and Dynamic Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST)

Static QST was measured using Mechanical Pain Threshold (MPT) and Pressure-Pain Threshold (PPT) for experimental pain measures, while dynamic QST was measured using mechanical temporal summation (TS) and Conditioned Pain Modulation (CPM). A standardized location on the forearm was used for MPT and temporal summation, while the low back region was used for the PPT and CPM procedures. The lower back location was determined by locating L4 and moving 3 cm toward the side of pain/more pain. For the forearm, the point between the mid third and proximal third sections in the lateral side of the volar region were chosen. A resting period of two minutes was set between each testing procedure.

##### MPT

This technique evaluated the threshold to produce pain. The Von Frey monofilaments (Von Frey monofilament, Touch-Test Sensory Evaluator, North Coast Medical, Morgan Hill, CA, USA) were used to assess patients’ ability to perceive nociceptive stimuli via the method of limits \[ [45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B45)\]. For this purpose, the examiner applied the filaments in an ascending standardized sequence over a specific standardized location in the subject’s low back (ipsilateral to side of most intensity low back pain or, if pain not well defined, contralateral to the dominant side) until pain was sensed by the participant. The participant was instructed to voice loud when pain was perceived and to keep her/his eyes closed during the application of the fibers. Fibers were applied perpendicularly to the skin and bowed for a period of approximately 1.5 seconds as described in \[ [46](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B46), [47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B47)\]. The smallest monofilament that produced pain was recorded.

##### PPT

For experimental PPT, an FDA approved algometer (JTech Commander Algometer Pain Evaluator—JTech Medical) with a 1 cm2 hard-rubber probe was used to apply a blunt increasing pressure at a standardized location in the patients’ low back area (most painful side or contralateral to the dominant side if pain was central in the spine). The recorded PPT value was the amount of pressure needed to provoke a pain sensation different from mere pressure or discomfort and was measured in pounds per centimeter squared (lbs/cm2). The subject was instructed to voice loud “stop” when pain was felt. At that moment, the probe was immediately removed. Pressure was applied perpendicularly to the skin at a constant rate of 2 lbs. per second until the PPT was identified. Three ascending pressure trials were performed and the final PPT was defined as the average of these trials \[ [48](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B48)\]. The subject was asked to keep their eyes closed for the PPT detections.

##### TS

To assess TS, a 6.65, 300 g Von Frey monofilament was applied to the standardized location in the subject’s lower back, as described in the PPT procedure (see above). The participant was asked to rate their pain on a 10 _cm_ line, as described above for the pain VAS questionnaire. Pain was rated twice: after a single stimulus was applied for ∼0.5 seconds, and after a series of 10 stimuli applied at ∼1-s intervals. An electronic metronome was used to assure the homogenous intervals between the consecutive stimuli. This procedure was repeated after thirty seconds and TS was defined as the average difference between the pain rating of the series of 10 stimuli and the rating of the single stimulus \[ [49](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B49)\]. Subject was instructed to keep their eyes closed during the test.

##### CPM

For CPM, the PPT was used as the test stimulus and cold pressor test as the conditioning stimulus. The use of this specific protocol is justified by its excellent reliability previously reported \[ [50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B50), [51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B51)\]. Pressure was delivered using the same protocol described above for the PPT evaluation. CPM was induced by the conditioning stimulus of having the participant immerse their hand (the one contralateral to the side where pressure was being applied according to previously mentioned PPT protocol) up to the wrist crease into a 10–12°C water bath for approximately one minute. After 60 seconds of the conditioning stimulus, the PPT procedure was conducted as described previously. Participants were instructed to keep their hand immersed in the water and keep their eyes closed during the entire procedure. CPM was defined as the difference in the average pressure threshold from the PPT trials and the pressure threshold from the CPM trials (PPT—CPM). As conventional for CPM reporting, negative values indicated that the subject tolerated a higher pressure in the trial with the cold-water conditioning stimulus than in the PPT trials.

#### Resting-State EEG Acquisition

Resting-state EEG was recorded continuously for 20 minutes, the first 10 minutes with eyes open and the remaining 10 minutes with eyes closed using a 20-channel EEG cap with 19 active electrodes (Enobio, Neuroelectrics, Cambridge, MA, USA). See [Figure  1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-F1) for active electrode placement.

EEG electrode placement.

Ground electrodes were placed over the mastoid region, and a sampling rate of 500 Hz was used. The impedance was kept under 10 kOhm. Recordings were performed in a quiet room. Patients were asked to sit in a comfortable position, have their sight directed naturally below the horizon line, not to move or talk, and relax as much as possible. The investigator made sure they did not fall asleep by observing the patient and verbally calling his attention if drowsiness was noticed. The analyses included 5 minutes of each recording to guarantee enough clean data after dealing with artifacts or potential moments of drowsiness.

We analyzed only the eyes-closed recordings, to reduce the alpha power attenuation (an important associated oscillation to chronic pain) as recommended by previous studies \[ [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B13), [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B14)\]. The data were then exported and analyzed offline using the EEGLab toolbox \[ [52](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B52)\] for MATLAB (MATLAB R2018a, The MathWorks Inc. Natick, MA, 2019). EEG was re-referenced to the average, then it was high-pass filtered with a 1 Hz Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filter to remove offset and trend and low-pass filtered with a FIR filter with 40 Hz cutoff frequency. The filtered EEG was visually analyzed by a clinical neurophysiologist to exclude the existence of any signal of drowsiness (attenuation of the alpha rhythm), epileptiform or any abnormal discharges prior to admission into full study (no epileptiform or abnormal discharges were found). This analysis was followed by a manual artifact detection and rejection and independent Component Analysis (ICA); finally, we removed the IC associated to artifacts and reconstructed the signal \[ [53](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B53)\].

The artifact-free EEG was processed using _pop\_spectopo_ EEGLab function with Fast Fourier Transformation with 5 s windows with 50% overlap. Absolute power (μV2) and relative power (power in a specific frequency range/total power from 1 to 40 Hz) were calculated for the following frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and the sub-bands: low alpha (8–10 Hz), high alpha (10–13 Hz), low beta (13–20 Hz) and high beta (20–30 Hz). Powers were calculated separately for each electrode in the central (C3, C4, Cz), parietal (P3, P4, Pz), and frontal (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, Fz) areas, which were selected as they are known to be involved in pain processing \[ [54](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B54)\]. For each area, the average values across channels were calculated.

### Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample. Dichotomous and categorical data were described in frequency and respective percentages while continuous variables were described by mean and standard deviation or by medians and interquartile ranges dependent on their distribution. To address our aims, exploratory linear regression analysis was used as described below. In addition, we have performed a sensitivity analysis using CPM values normalized to the PPT values.

#### CPM and Pain

For our primary aim, univariate linear regression was used. VAS\_Avg was used as the dependent variable and CPM was used as independent variable. Both variables were treated as continuous variables. No other variables were considered for this model as the aim was to explore the direct relationship between CPM response and self-reported pain intensity. Values greater than 3 standard deviations (SD) away from the mean scores of the dependent or independent variables were labeled as outliers. The assumption of linearity was assessed by visually comparing the scatterplot of each independent variable and a superimposed regression line plot. The assumption of homoscedasticity was checked by visual inspection of the scatterplot of the standardized predicted values and standardized residuals \[ [55](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B55)\]. Residuals were tested for normality using histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk normality test \[ [56](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B56)\]. Durbin Watson estimates, tolerance values, Cook’s distance, and Standardized DFBeta values were used for analysis of regression diagnostics such as multicollinearity and influential cases.

#### EEG and CPM

Univariate linear regressions models were built to explore the association between CPM response and the individual EEG variables. For these models, CPM was used as a dependent variable. EEG variables of each frequency band at the frontal, central, and parietal areas were used as independent variables. No other independent variables were considered as the aim was to explore the direct relationship between CPM response and each EEG frequency band at the mentioned brain areas. All variables were treated as continuous variables. The models were built following a procedure like the described above.

#### EEG and Pain

Multiple linear regressions models were built to explore the association between the EEG variables and self-reported pain intensity. Pain (VAS\_Avg) was used as a dependent variable. All EEG variables (48), the VAMS for anxiety, stress, depression, and sleepiness (4), and all the QST variables (4) were considered at first as exploratory independent variables. All the variables were treated as continuous variables. The first step, to best select the variables for a subsequent best model, univariate linear models were created with each independent variable to detect significant covariates for an alpha level of 0.2. Thus, a total of 56 univariate models were created. Variables that were not significant at the univariate models were eliminated. With that, we diminished the number of variables for the following analysis. Less variables to build the final models would require less tests needed to achieve a final model, thus, lower the chances of type one error with multiple testing \[ [57](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B57)\]. Of the 56 variables, 42 variables were eliminated ( _P_ > .02). As a second step, a multivariate model was created with all the variables that were significant ( _P_ < .2) in the univariate models according to the EEG band frequency. For example, a Delta (EEG band) model was created using with all the EEG variables from the Delta band that were significant at the first step in addition to all other variables that were also significant. The Delta, Alpha, Theta models had initially seven, ten, and six variables, respectively. Thirdly, the regression coefficients were then checked for significance and those with a _P_ values >.05 were excluded from the model in a backward elimination approach leaving only the significant variables ( _P_ < .05) to create the final model for each EEG frequency band. We have chosen not to use a stepwise multiple regression method due to the exploratory nature of this analysis and the known limitations of the stepwise regression method \[ [58](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B58)\]. Also due to the exploratory nature of our study, the conservative approach to correct the significance level for multiple testing was not performed. Due to the limited sample size (30), we allowed no more than three variables in the final model. The preliminary models, before step three, included the following variables: 1) Delta Model: PPT, VAMS (anxiety, stress, depression, and sleepiness), relative delta power in the central area, and absolute delta power in the frontal area; 2) Alpha Model: PPT, VAMS (anxiety, stress, depression, and sleepiness), low alpha absolute power in the frontal area, absolute alpha power in the frontal area, low alpha relative power in the central area, relative alpha power in the central area, and high alpha relative power in the central area; 3) Theta Model: PPT, VAMS (anxiety, stress, depression, and sleepiness), and relative theta power in the central area. The Akaike’s information criteria was used to select the variables that would result in the best fit \[ [59](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B59), [60](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9125498/#pnab293-B60)\]. Durbin Watson estimates and Cook’s distance values were used for analysis of regression diagnostics such as residual autocorrelation and influential cases. All analysis was performed using statistical software STATA version 15 (StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.).

Age and gender were explored as biological variables that could potentially confound all final models. Once the final model was determined, we added these variables as covariates, and if not significant, they were excluded from the models.

### CPM and Pain

From the 30 patients, twenty-one were included in the analysis of CPM and Pain. Seven patients were excluded during the run-in phase of the main trial thus not included in this analysis (as they did not perform the CPM assessment), one subject dropped out before QST data were collected (due to time commitment) and one outlier was identified for the CPM variable with a value of −6.3 and removed from the analysis. A univariate linear regression model was generated using pain VAS\_Avg as the dependent variable. Visual analysis of the scatterplots as well as the residuals confirmed the assumption of linearity and homoscedasticity. No influential cases were detected according to the Cook’s distance values all >1 assuring no influential cases. The Durbin Watson statistic showed no value <1 or >3 for the model, indicating that the assumption of independent errors was met. Although there was a relationship between increased pain to reduced CPM, the CPM regression coefficient was not statistically significant, and CPM was not able to significantly explain the variability in pain scores in our sample. Age and gender were not significant and therefore not present in the model.
